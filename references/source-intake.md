# Supplied source intake

Treat an article, paper, URL, transcript, or document as input material, not as
a page template or unquestioned truth.

## Read and extract

Read the complete relevant source. If access is blocked, try a legitimate
print view, feed, abstract, attached copy, or another version. If the material
still cannot be read, ask the user to provide it rather than guessing.

Extract:

- The central question or tension.
- The source's thesis and major branches.
- Every factual, numeric, historical, and mechanism claim that may survive into
  the new explainer.
- Primary sources cited by the source.
- Concepts with causal, spatial, sequential, or quantitative structure.
- Limitations, uncertainty, and competing views.

## Re-author rather than convert

Choose one learning spine. Classify source material as:

- Spine: essential to the one-sentence objective.
- Support: background, evidence, or limitations that strengthen the spine.
- Omit: valid material that would diffuse this explainer.

Do not preserve the source's section order merely because it exists. Design a
new learning sequence around what the learner should discover. Use original
wording and structure, quote only when the exact words matter, and link the
source. Never reproduce a copyrighted article or distinctive presentation at
substitution-level similarity.

## Evidence hierarchy

Follow cited primary sources when possible and verify claims against them. A
provided article can supply the narrative lead, but the original paper,
official documentation, dataset, law, or implementation should settle factual
questions. Mark opinions from the source as opinions.

When the user provides several sources, name one as the narrative spine and
the others as supporting or contrasting evidence. For mixed topic research,
use current primary sources to correct or update the supplied material.
